The Health Sector Crisis in Pakistan, From the Inside

An opinion piece by Dr. Muzamil Fiaz, MBBS

I graduated into a healthcare system that is, on paper, producing more doctors than it ever has — Pakistan's medical schools turn out somewhere between 20,000 and 33,000 new doctors a year. And yet 2025 saw the largest recorded outflow of doctors leaving the country in the nation's history, an estimated 3,800 to 4,000 formally emigrating in a single year. That contradiction is the whole crisis in one sentence: we are training people faster than we are giving them a reason to stay.

What's actually pushing people out

It isn't just salary, though that's real — postgraduate training routinely demands roughly 80-hour work weeks for pay that doesn't match the hours or the responsibility. It's the mismatch between what five years of rigorous training prepares you to expect, and what the system on the other side is capable of offering: outdated equipment, wards that are structurally understaffed, and administrative environments where doing the job well is harder than it should be. A doctor who trains abroad, even briefly, sees a system that rewards initiative rather than just endurance, and the comparison is hard to unsee.

The gap isn't evenly spread

Doctor numbers look almost adequate next to other cadres. Pakistan's real shortages are in nursing and allied health — the workforce that actually keeps a ward running day to day. Some estimates put the nursing shortfall above 90 percent of what international standards would call adequate. A hospital can have enough doctors on paper and still fail patients because there aren't enough nurses to safely staff a shift. That's the part of the crisis that rarely makes headlines, because "doctor shortage" is a simpler story to tell than "we don't have enough of nearly every other kind of health worker."

And this isn't abstract. Reporting from Rawalpindi this year described maternity patients sharing a single hospital bed due to space shortages, and a fully-built 500-bed hospital sitting unused and deteriorating for over a decade despite billions of rupees already spent on it. Infrastructure spending without the staffing and management to use it is money that never reaches a patient.

We keep treating this as a doctor shortage. It's actually a systems problem wearing a doctor shortage as its most visible symptom.

My actual opinion

Retention has to be treated as seriously as production. That means postgraduate training that respects working hours, career paths that reward staying rather than only rewarding those who leave and send money back, and serious investment in the nursing and allied health workforce that keeps any hospital functional. Building more medical colleges without fixing what happens to their graduates afterward is solving the wrong end of the pipeline. Every doctor who leaves isn't lost to laziness or disloyalty — they're a rational person responding to a system that hasn't given them a reason to stay, and until that changes, we'll keep training talent for other countries' hospitals instead of our own.
