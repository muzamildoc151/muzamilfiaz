Building a Business in Pakistan Right Now

An opinion piece by Dr. Muzamil Fiaz

Pakistan's startup scene spent 2023 and early 2024 in what can only be described as a funding drought — disclosed startup funding fell by roughly 77 percent from its 2022 level, and early-stage seed rounds nearly disappeared entirely. By 2025, things started moving again: equity funding climbed to a little over $36 million for the year, and the best quarter in nearly three years pulled in around $58 million on its own. That's real recovery. It's also still a fraction of the $300-plus million years the ecosystem saw back in 2021 and 2022.

As someone building small digital projects on the side of medicine, I read that recovery with mixed feelings. The momentum is genuine — fintech keeps pulling in the bulk of investment because the country still has well over a hundred million financially underserved adults, and healthtech and e-commerce are finally getting attention as problem-focused categories rather than copycat ideas. Female-founded startups had a real breakout year too, pulling in a noticeably larger share of total funding than before. None of that is small.

The part that doesn't get fixed by a good headline

What the recovery numbers hide is concentration. A handful of larger, later-stage deals are doing most of the lifting, while early-stage founders — the ones without an existing network of investors — are still struggling to get funded at all. Average deal sizes have gone up while the number of deals has stayed low. That's not a healthy ecosystem, that's a narrow one. And Pakistan still hasn't produced a unicorn despite the ecosystem's total value crossing several billion dollars, which tells you something about how much of that value is still on paper rather than in scaled, exited companies.

What actually holds founders back

It's not usually the idea. It's the operating environment around the idea — a volatile rupee that makes any dollar-denominated cost plan obsolete within a year, regulatory processes that assume a founder has a lawyer on retainer, and a local venture capital base too thin to fund a company past its first product. Add in political instability that makes multi-year planning genuinely difficult, and you get founders who are excellent at surviving and mediocre at scaling — because scaling was never really the environment's priority.

Pakistan doesn't have a shortage of entrepreneurs. It has a shortage of infrastructure that lets an entrepreneur stop firefighting long enough to build.

My actual opinion

I think the reforms happening now — startup-specific tax classifications, National Incubation Centers, State Bank rate cuts easing the cost of capital — are the right direction, but they're policy-level fixes to what is often an execution-level problem. A founder in Lahore or Karachi today needs less friction on day-to-day things: faster company registration, dependable electricity for their own operations, and access to even small institutional capital before they're "investable" by bigger funds. Until that early-stage gap closes, we'll keep seeing promising startups plateau right at the point where they'd otherwise start compounding.
