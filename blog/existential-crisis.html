The Modern Era's Existential Crisis

An opinion piece by Dr. Muzamil Fiaz

There's a particular kind of tiredness in young people today that isn't about workload. It's about meaning — a quiet sense that despite being more connected, informed and "productive" than any generation before, life doesn't feel like it's adding up to anything. I don't think that's dramatic. I think it's an accurate description of what happens when a generation is handed infinite information and very little guidance on what to do with it.

Comparison at industrial scale

Social media didn't invent envy or self-doubt, but it industrialised them. Studies on Pakistani university students link heavy social media use directly to higher stress, anxiety and depression, and a meaningful share of teens report feeling low specifically after using it. What's happening isn't really "social" media in the way that word implies — it's a constant, curated stream of other people's highlight reels, arriving fast enough that a young person's own ordinary life starts to feel like a failure by comparison. The fear-of-missing-out effect is well documented, and it doesn't discriminate by country.

Pakistan's specific version of this

What makes it sharper here is that global existential anxiety collides with very local pressure. Pakistani youth carry heavy academic expectations, intense competition for a shrinking pool of stable jobs, and family expectations around career, marriage and success timelines that leave little room to figure out who you actually are before you're expected to have already become it. Add a national brain-drain conversation that quietly tells every ambitious young person "the real version of your future is somewhere else," and you get a generation questioning not just what they want, but whether wanting anything here is even worth it.

I've heard the brain drain described as a crisis of trust between the state and its youth, more than a simple financial calculation. Universities reward memorising over questioning, research funding is thin, and the students who go abroad discover systems that reward curiosity instead of conformity. That contrast doesn't just cost the country talent — it costs the people who leave a certain kind of belonging they don't always get back.

The existential crisis isn't "nothing matters." It's "so much is visible now that nothing I can actually reach feels like enough."

My actual opinion

I don't think the answer is switching off the internet or pretending global comparison isn't happening — that ship has sailed. I think it's building a slower, more honest relationship with ambition: measuring your life against your own five years ago instead of a stranger's best day, and accepting that a meaningful life doesn't have to look like anyone else's version of success, including the one trending this week. For Pakistan specifically, that also means giving young people real reasons to invest their future here — research funding, workplaces that reward initiative, spaces where curiosity isn't punished — because a generation will only stop looking elsewhere for meaning once looking here stops feeling like settling.
