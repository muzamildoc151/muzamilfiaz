Marriage in Pakistan: What the Statistics Miss

An opinion piece by Dr. Muzamil Fiaz

Marriage in Pakistan is still overwhelmingly a family decision before it's a personal one. Roughly eight or nine out of every ten marriages in the country are arranged rather than chosen independently, and that single fact shapes almost everything else people argue about when they talk about "marriage problems" here — compatibility, in-laws, timing, expectations.

What surprises people who assume arranged marriage is an outdated or unhappy system is that satisfaction surveys don't back that up cleanly. Several studies comparing arranged and love marriages in Pakistan report arranged couples describing higher day-to-day satisfaction than love-marriage couples, and some data even suggests divorce is more common among love marriages here, not less — the opposite of the pattern many expect. I don't think that means arranged marriage is inherently superior. I think it means the two systems fail for different reasons, and we tend to only talk about one of them.

Where arranged marriage actually struggles

The honest problems with arranged marriage aren't about the concept itself — they're about consent and timing. Early or coerced marriages, still present in parts of rural Sindh and Balochistan despite legal restrictions, produce measurably higher dissolution rates, because the people in them were never given real choice to begin with. That's not a case against family involvement in marriage. It's a case against removing agency from the person who has to live in it.

Where love marriage actually struggles

Family courts and lawyers describe a recognisable pattern behind failed love marriages: partners misrepresenting their education, income or background before marriage, and social media replacing the older habit of having elders mediate a disagreement before it escalates. A generation raised on curated online lives is walking into marriage with inflated expectations of what a partner and a relationship should look like — and that gap between expectation and reality is where a lot of urban divorces are actually coming from.

The debate shouldn't be "arranged versus love." It should be "informed versus uninformed" — and consent, not tradition, is the dividing line.

My actual opinion

I don't think Pakistan needs to pick a side in the arranged-versus-love debate. I think we need better inputs into both systems: real premarital conversations instead of a single formal meeting, financial and expectation transparency before a rishta is finalised, and family involvement that still leaves room for a person to say no. Divorce rates are rising across the country, in cities faster than villages, largely because urban women now have more access to courts and legal awareness than a generation ago — that's not moral decline, that's a justice system finally being used by people it used to fail quietly.

Marriage isn't broken because it's arranged or because it's chosen. It's strained wherever the people inside it were given a ceremony instead of a real conversation.
